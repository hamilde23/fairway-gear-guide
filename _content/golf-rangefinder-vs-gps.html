A laser rangefinder is useful for measuring to a visible target. A GPS watch or app is useful for seeing mapped distances and the shape of a hole. Neither is automatically the better choice: choose the one that answers your usual on-course questions with the least interruption.

The practical comparison

OptionMain useCheck before choosing
Laser rangefinderDistance to a target you can see and identifyDisplay readability, target acquisition, steadiness, battery and case access
GPS watchQuick mapped distances from your wristCourse coverage, outdoor visibility, fit, charging and enabled features
GPS phone appA larger map and hole overviewPhone handling, battery use, course maps, required connectivity and paid features

Features vary by model and subscription. Read the specification for the exact product you are considering rather than assuming every device in the category works the same way.

Choose a laser when you want a visible target

A laser can help when you want to check the flag or another object you can sight. The practical challenge is identifying the intended target. Try the display and controls before buying, especially if you wear glasses or find it difficult to hold a small device steady.

If a reading seems implausible, check it again against the scene and any course markings. A number is useful only when you know what was measured. Keep the device somewhere you can reach without holding up your routine.

Choose GPS when you want an overview

A GPS display can help you think about the green as an area, rather than treating the flag as the only target. Depending on the device, it may also show mapped hazards or lay-up information. Verify that your regular courses are covered and that the features you want are included.

Mapped distances are not the same as a fresh laser reading to today’s flag position. Check the selected hole and your position when a display looks wrong, and review the provider’s update process for courses that have changed.

Watch or phone app?

A watch keeps information close to hand, but the screen is small and you need to be comfortable wearing it while swinging. A phone offers more display space, though retrieving, unlocking and storing it can interrupt the round. Try your existing phone setup before deciding you need a separate device.

Consider whether you want a distance tool or a broader shot-tracking system. Extra features have value only if you will use them and are comfortable with the setup and ongoing costs.

When using both makes sense

The tools can answer different questions: GPS for the layout, then a laser for a visible target. That combination can be useful, but carrying both is optional. If one tool already gives you enough information to choose a sensible shot, adding a second may only add another step.

Check the full cost and the routine

  • Purchase price plus any subscription needed for the features you want.
  • Battery replacement or charging requirements across a full round.
  • Storage, weather protection and ease of use with your usual clothing.
  • Course coverage, updates and compatibility with your phone.
  • Warranty, support and return terms.

For competition, check the event rules and the permitted modes before using any device. For example, a device offering slope-adjusted information should not be assumed acceptable in every configuration. The R&A’s Rule 4 is the starting point for equipment use; ask the committee about the event’s specific conditions.

Try this before buying

During your next round, notice which information you actually wish you had. If it is usually distance to a visible flag, try a laser. If it is the layout of unfamiliar holes, investigate GPS coverage. If your current tool answers both well enough, keep it.
