Bring your current driver, the ball you normally play if the facility permits it, your golf shoes and a clear description of what you want to improve. The goal of a fitting is a repeatable tee shot and a specification you can reproduce, not one impressive swing.

Before the appointment

Ask what the session includes, how long it lasts, which brands are available, whether it is indoors or outdoors and whether there is an obligation to buy. Find out if the fee includes a written specification and any follow-up adjustment. Tell the fitter your budget at the start.

  • Your current driver, including its present loft and weight settings.
  • Your usual golf shoes and glove.
  • Your normal ball model, if the facility allows it.
  • A description of your common miss and the shots you want to improve.
  • Any relevant launch-monitor results, with the conditions noted.
  • A budget that includes the complete build, not just the head.

Define the problem in plain language

“I need fewer tee shots that leave me blocked out” is a more useful starting point than “I want a tour shaft.” Explain whether the problem is a wide miss, inconsistent contact, too little carry or a flight you struggle to control. Your handicap is context, not a specification.

Ask the fitter how each proposed change addresses that problem. If several variables change at once, have them explain the trade-off so you understand what you are comparing.

Record a baseline with your own driver

Warm up before collecting comparison shots. Hit a representative group with your current club, then keep those results as the baseline. Include ordinary mishits: they are part of your game. Device misreads can be excluded, but distinguish those from real poor shots.

Return to your own driver during the session. That helps you notice whether an apparent improvement came from a new club, getting warmed up or a temporary change in your swing.

What to compare beyond total distance

ObservationQuestion to ask
Typical carryHow far does the ball usually fly, rather than on the single best swing?
Left-to-right spreadDoes the pattern give me more playable next shots?
Strike locationCan I repeatedly find a useful part of the face?
Launch and spinDo these numbers support the flight I need at my ball speed?
Poorer strikesWhat happens when my contact or delivery is less consistent?
Comfort and confidenceCan I make my normal swing without forcing a different tempo?

There is no single launch-and-spin target that fits every golfer. Ask the fitter to explain the numbers in the context of your delivery, ball speed and playing conditions. Treat simulated rollout as an estimate; compare carry separately.

Write down the complete build

Before ordering, copy this specification list into your notes. A model name and a flex letter alone do not describe the fitted club.

  • Head model and stated loft.
  • Hosel setting and movable-weight positions.
  • Shaft manufacturer, model, weight designation and flex.
  • Playing length and any agreed build adjustments.
  • Grip model, size and build-up details.
  • Final price, lead time, return terms and follow-up arrangements.

When an upgrade is worth considering

Look for an improvement that survives repeated comparisons and solves the problem you described. A modest distance change with a much more usable pattern may matter more than a longer best shot. If the result is unclear, ask for the data and take time to decide.

Can a fitting fix a slice?

A fitting can explore equipment that makes your ball flight more manageable, but it does not guarantee a swing correction. Ask the fitter to distinguish an equipment issue from a delivery issue and explain whether coaching should be part of the plan.

Should a beginner get fitted?

A beginner can use a fitting to check basic suitability, comfort and length without committing to the most expensive option. Explain your experience and budget. Ask whether an adjustable, used or existing club can meet your needs while your swing develops.
